		One does not need skill to make art. 
It is said that artists are excavators that accept the adventure. 
No need for exuberant thirst to avenge either. 
All one needs is the need to do it. 
For painters, you don’t even need to know how to paint; you don’t even need to paint, they will still call you a painter. 
This is true, because painting does not need a brush; it just happens to be the tool of the day. 
It does not need fresh paint. 
You can use old paint, even paint from other people’s paintings. 

Every year, approximately millions of paintings are thrown out to the garbage fields. 
Within a span of 10 hours, half of those paintings are discovered by the Wizard of the Crow himself, or really anyone that happens to take a stroll through the underground sewers. 
Some have no choice but to love the caves where they made their discoveries. Yes, caves littered with shy and powerful minerals. 
Anyway, the artists, unidentified in their title, had the title 'Unidentified Artist' engraved underneath their discovery by equally unidentified artists in clean white gloves. 
The artists don’t need skill, just a need to do it. 
Not the reason, although maybe it’s the same thing. 
The hesitation here is that “reason,” the word, carries the same letter r from “rationality.” 
And art has nothing to do with rationality, at least as it is presented to her.

Having said all this, she is different. 
On top of her drive through foggy bridges, she also has skill. 
She can paint or draw or sculpt anything on the spot, I have witnessed it.

Rationality did not peek through any of the answers as you glided through the landscape of two twins, different-colored hair, different eye contacts, heads at each other’s feet inside a womb. 
I talked to her once, many times. 
I told her about some trauma that had befallen me during my childhood. 
Like any good listener, she let out a quiet breath that filled the first floor studio with big windows and indoor birds, with a permission that helped me tie the strings.

“I am an adult now,” I declared. 
My mother and sisters jokingly called me ugly when I was young. 
I went outside and the neighbors called me handsome. 
Then they asked me if I was ready to be the man now that my father had died. 
I told them that my father had died a long time ago, when I was two, but I haven’t yet felt like the man. 
So I obeyed my mother’s and sisters’ command and went to fetch water as they made me food, and they told the neighbors not to give me a big head.

Anyway, here I was, years later, lost as a single winged bird in her studio, the twin with white hair staring straight at me. 
I felt seen.
